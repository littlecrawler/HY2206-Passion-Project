My journey and reflection

Why I chose money, how the website changed, and what HY2206 helped me see.

Watch the project videos

Who is this for?

Students who use money every day but may be new to Chinese history.

What I want to share

Monetary change was shaped by practical needs, political choices and social acceptance. A new form could join older ones, rather than replace them.

From an interest to a website

I first considered two topics: the development of money and the development of architecture. I chose money because it connects ancient objects with something familiar in daily life. Song jiaozi（交子）was my starting point, but I wanted to understand the longer history around it.

A website let me put objects, historical writing and my own explanation together. Based on the feedback, I reconsidered the website in this order: audience, message, then form. I focused on students encountering this history for the first time.

Learning to simplify

I initially wanted visible merchants and a game. As the prototype grew, its purpose became harder to follow. Long explanations and too many small headings also asked the reader to do too much at once.

I chose a deck of picture cards so the reader can focus on one period at a time. The first version became too much like a short history summary, so I brought object exploration, comparisons and historical texts back into the cards. I later added a three-dimensional museum while keeping the flashcards and a reading view, so visitors can choose how they explore. I now give the modes different roles: the museum invites close looking, the cards ask focused questions, and the history pages bring together the fuller narrative and historical sources.

Reflections on HY2206

Who sets the rules?

The course’s discussion of Qin standardisation helped me see how political authority could shape everyday money. The Han Salt and Iron Debate raised a related question: how far should government intervene in economic life? I began to look at coins alongside the decisions about who could produce them and set their standards. [16]

What do people trust?

The Song lectures connected commercial growth with monetary innovation. [17] This led me to think about the psychology of trust: do people trust the material, the issuer, or the expectation that someone else will accept it?

The History of the Song describes jiaozi issuers who could not repay what they owed. [1] [6] I read this as a problem of confidence in a promise. Carrying paper was easier, but people still had to judge whether that promise would be kept.

How my understanding changed

I began by looking at the shapes and materials of money. I now also look at the people behind it: who issues it, who accepts it, and why they trust it. The change from coins to paper makes more sense to me when I consider both convenience and confidence.

Building the website changed how I communicate history, too. A long page could contain plenty of information and still be difficult to follow. Organising the material into galleries and cards taught me to give visitors one clear question and an object or source to examine at a time.
