Who is this for?

University students, especially non-history majors who use electronic payments but have little background in imperial Chinese monetary history. Visitors do not need to know the dynasties or read classical Chinese before beginning.

The opening starts with an everyday experience: making a payment. The historical route then introduces unfamiliar objects and institutions through questions that the audience can understand.

What should the audience take away?

Monetary forms changed in response to practical needs, political choices and social acceptance. New forms often coexisted with older ones.

After exploring, a visitor should be able to explain one reason a monetary form changed, one reason different forms remained in use together, and one limit of comparing imperial money with digital payments.

Why a website?

Museum photographs encourage close observation. A one-minute cloth purchase lets the reader compare coins and paper, see whether a trader accepts payment, and understand the trade-off between weight and trust. Historical and literary excerpts let the reader inspect the evidence. Personal reflection explains how the project's questions and presentation developed.

The visitor can follow the chronology or enter through a particular object, interaction or text. Each format serves a specific part of the message.

The historical route

  • Prologue: selected early shells and shaped money.
  • Qin–Han: banliang coins（半兩錢）, standardisation, inscriptions and changing rules.
  • Song Sichuan: iron cash（鐵錢）, jiaozi（交子）, repayment and official issuance. The deepest interactive case.
  • Ming–Qing: a Ming note's coin imagery, followed by distinct Qing examples of copper and silver use.
  • Today: a PayNow comparison distinguishing money from a service that transfers funds.

This is a thematic selection, not a complete account of every dynasty or a claim that Song paper money directly led to PayNow.

Two course-based analyses

State power and common standards. Week 4's discussion of Qin standardisation connects a coin to administration and exchange. A historical passage about four-zhu coins retaining the banliang inscription complicates the assumption that a stable appearance means stable rules.

Convenience and institutional dependence. Week 8's treatment of monetary innovation and government involvement in commerce helps frame the Song case. Paper could ease a physical burden while raising questions about issuers' obligations and acceptance.

Personal journey and reflection

The reflection follows documented decisions: choosing money over architecture, broadening a Song-focused concept, adding visual characters and textual evidence, and responding to feedback by starting with the audience. It connects the project to the Salt and Iron Debate and the student's experience programming a classroom board game.

The concluding question is: better for whom, for which transaction, and under which conditions? This makes development a question about people, institutions and evidence.

Evidence and authorship

The site distinguishes museum objects, historical texts, literary representations and interpretation. The Song official history, Kaifeng memoir and Hangzhou lyric have different perspectives and settings. The game dialogue is fictional; its AI-generated scenes are interpretive illustrations.

The museum photographs are CC0 images by Gary Lee Todd and BabelStone, with visible credits. They are not presented as the student's own photographs. No personal video has been supplied or embedded.

AI assisted with discovery, wording, working translations, code, diagrams and game imagery. The personal reflection is an assisted draft grounded in the student's stated choices and existing course reflection, for review in their own voice. The full scholarly chapter and critical editions still require consultation.
