My journey and reflection

Why I chose money, how the website changed, and what HY2206 helped me see.

Watch the project videos

Who is this for?

Students who use money every day but may be new to Chinese history.

What I want to share

Monetary change was shaped by practical needs, political choices and social acceptance. A new form could join older ones, rather than replace them.

From an interest to a website

I first considered two topics: the development of money and the development of architecture. I chose money because it connects ancient objects with something familiar in daily life. Song jiaozi（交子）was my starting point, but I wanted to understand the longer history around it.

A website let me put objects, historical writing and my own explanation together. My professor’s feedback changed the order of my thinking: audience first, then message, then form. That helped me focus on students encountering this history for the first time.

Learning to simplify

I initially wanted visible merchants and a game. As the prototype grew, its purpose became harder to follow. Long explanations and too many small headings also asked the reader to do too much at once.

I chose a deck of picture cards so the reader can focus on one period at a time. The first version became too much like a short history summary, so I brought object exploration, comparisons and historical texts back into the cards. I later added a three-dimensional museum while keeping the flashcards and a reading view, so visitors can choose how they explore. The challenge is to make each interaction contribute to understanding while keeping the reading manageable.

Two ideas from HY2206

State power could shape everyday money.

The course’s discussion of Qin standardisation helped me see coins as more than objects. A shared standard also expressed authority. The Han Salt and Iron Debate raised a related question: how far should government intervene in economic life? These ideas gave me a way to connect monetary rules with wider political choices. [16]

Convenience depended on institutions.

The Song lectures connected commercial growth with monetary innovation and debates over government involvement. Jiaozi made carrying value easier, but paper alone could not guarantee repayment. I therefore came to ask who issued money, who accepted it and what supported the promise. [1] [6] [17]

What I take away

I now ask, “Better for whom, for which transaction, and under which conditions?” A lighter form could be convenient without making every payment easier. Several forms could remain useful together.

I also learned to keep different kinds of evidence distinct. A coin shows a material object; an official history explains institutions; a poem evokes a city. Reading them together is valuable when I also recognise what each can—and cannot—tell me.
