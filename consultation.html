The changing forms
of money in China.

An interactive historical exhibit about why monetary forms changed, what problems they addressed, and why different forms continued to coexist.

Consultation: 2 November 2026Status: working conceptMedium: interactive website

The research question

Why did the forms of money change in imperial China, and how did new forms address practical needs while creating new questions of acceptance and regulation?

The project follows selected turning points rather than every dynasty. Its proposed argument is that monetary development involved both change and coexistence: a new instrument could address one problem without replacing every earlier form.

Three chapters, with a short beginning and ending

Chapter 01 · Qin & Han

Metal coins

Inspect a coin's shape, inscription, and issuing rules. Compare standardisation with a historical passage in which the banliang name remains while the stated weight changes.

Chapter 02 · Song

Paper money

The deepest interactive case: a fictional Sichuan merchant explores the burden of iron cash, private jiaozi, issuer reliability, and state involvement in the 1020s.

Chapter 03 · Ming & Qing

Coexisting forms

A Ming note refers to coins; a Qing activity compares copper and silver in different transactions. These are distinct snapshots, not one uniform system.

Prologue: a brief introduction to shells and shaped coins. Epilogue: cash, account balances, and a familiar PayNow payment, with the distinction between money and a transfer service made explicit.

The proposed historical focus is imperial China. The early prologue and modern comparison frame that focus. Yuan and other periods are outside the detailed cases; the route is not a complete history or a claim of direct descent from jiaozi to digital payments.

The one-minute introduction

I would like to create an interactive website about the changing forms of money in China. My interest is in why people used different forms of money, what problems each form addressed, and why older forms often remained in use. The website would have three historical chapters: Qin and Han metal coins, Song paper money, and monetary coexistence in the Ming and Qing. The Song chapter would contain the main merchant game, while the other chapters would use object inspection and transaction activities. Historical passages, literature, and a museum object would help visitors test the explanations. A short modern comparison would connect the history to familiar payments. I also plan to add my own recorded commentary to explain my research and interpretation.

A five-minute demonstration route

  1. Show the timeline. Explain the three selected turning points and the limits of the scope.
  2. Inspect a coin. Select “Inscription,” then compare the diagram with the passage from the Records of the Grand Historian.
  3. Play one Song encounter. Offer iron cash or a paper note, hear the trader's reply, and open the historical field note. Show how it links to a source passage.
  4. Test coexistence. Switch between everyday goods and a larger trade in the Qing activity; point to the linked Ming museum object.
  5. Close with the modern comparison. Distinguish a transfer service from money, then show the source list and planned personal commentary.

Historical and literary evidence

Evidence is part of the visitor route, with short excerpts, working English translations, necessary traditional Chinese text in parentheses, and explanations of what each source can support.

  1. Records of the Grand Historian（史記）, vol. 30: a Han account of monetary policy complicates the relationship between a coin's inscription and its stated weight.
  2. History of the Song（宋史）, vol. 181, Siku Quanshu version: three excerpts address the burden of iron cash, repayment failures, and an issuing institution. This is a later Yuan compilation.
  3. Dreams of the Splendour of the Eastern Capital（東京夢華錄） and Liu Yong's Wang hai chao（望海潮）: a memoir and a lyric offer perspectives on urban life and prosperity. Their Kaifeng and Hangzhou settings are distinguished from Sichuan.
  4. A British Museum Ming banknote: the object and its catalogue description invite analysis of references to copper cash. Museum and university resources support the wider chronological context.

The Song reading room includes a three-question exercise about the limits of evidence. Literary representations are not treated as transaction records, and modern sources are not evidence for imperial institutions.

Research and production status

The demo contains all three chapters, a clickable timeline, two illustrated Song scenes, a source-reading activity, and the modern payment comparison. It is a consultation prototype rather than the final assessed product.

The scholarly foundation currently includes the accessible summary of Richard von Glahn's chapter on Song Sichuan; the full chapter has not yet been reviewed. Museum and university teaching resources support the new chapters. Further scholarship, critical editions, and the student's own interpretation remain essential before submission.

The merchant, dialogue, transaction situations, and receipt are fictional teaching devices. Currency drawings are schematic and not reproductions of particular surviving objects. No historical prices, conversion rates, or measured coin weights are assigned to the drawings.

AI assisted with source discovery, working translations, draft wording, implementation, schematic drawings, and two interpretive game scenes. Research and image prompts are retained in the repository. The final explanation should identify that assistance and explain the student's decisions.

After consultation: refine the scope, deepen the research, review translations, record personal commentary, test with readers, and prepare the required separate 500–800-word explanation and bibliography.
