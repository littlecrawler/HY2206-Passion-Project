Prologue / Before imperial unification

Money had
many shapes.

Early Chinese monetary objects included cowrie money（貝幣） and, later, cast forms such as spade money（布幣） and knife money（刀幣）. Different forms belonged to different settings; this display does not present a universal sequence of replacements. [9] [10]

01 / Qin & Han · Metal money

One familiar shape.
Changing monetary rules.

After Qin unification, round coins with square holes became a lasting form. Their familiar appearance did not make standards or issuing arrangements timeless. [9]

WHAT CAN THE SHAPE TELL US?

A shared, recognisable form

The round coin with a square hole became characteristic of imperial Chinese cash. A common form is evidence of standardisation; it does not establish that every coin had the same weight or value. [9]

02 / Song · A regional turning point

A local problem.
A consequential idea.

Now move to Sichuan（四川）, around the beginning of the Song dynasty（宋朝）. Heavy iron cash（鐵錢）made moving value physically demanding. Privately issued jiaozi（交子） offered another way to transact. [1]

Paper changed what a merchant might carry. It also raised questions about the issuer, repayment, and acceptance. Explore those questions in the exhibit's central game.

A focused case study of Song Sichuan; conditions elsewhere in China varied.

The “third watch” is a traditional division of the night. The surrounding passage describes food sellers and markets reopening before dawn.

In his preface, Meng explains that he is recalling the capital after leaving it. The work preserves observations of urban life through the lens of memory and loss.

Chapter 02 / What changed?

The paper was only
part of the story.

The useful question is how a promise
became usable in everyday exchange.

01

Movement

Growing trade made the burden of carrying metal money a practical problem. Paper offered a way to move value more conveniently. [2]

02

Obligations

Some private issuers of Sichuan jiaozi became insolvent. The reliability of the issuer mattered alongside the usefulness of the document. [1]

03

Institutions

The state took over issuance in the 1020s. Jiaozi's later role involved government revenues and payments as well as commerce. [1]

↳ Coins also depended on shared rules and acceptance. This is a story about changing arrangements of trust, not the invention of trust itself.

03 / Ming & Qing · Coexisting forms

New money did not
make metal disappear.

Two later snapshots complicate a simple story of progress from coins to paper. Follow an object from the Ming, then explore a pattern from the Qing.

SNAPSHOT AMing1368–1644

A paper note that points back to coins

The British Museum's Great Ming Treasure Note（大明寶鈔） carries a denomination expressed in strings of cash and describes circulation alongside bronze coins. The museum's account also traces the growing role of silver. [5]

When viewing the object, look for the pictured strings of coins. What does this reference to metal help a paper note communicate?

Snapshot B / Qing · 1644–1911

A different transaction,
a different monetary need.

Copper cash（銅錢） served everyday exchange; silver（白銀） was important for larger payments and taxation. Select a situation to explore this broad pattern. [12]

EVERYDAY EXCHANGE

Small purchases, copper cash

Copper coins（銅錢） were commonly used for everyday transactions. The useful question is which denominations and forms a local market would accept.

These are teaching scenarios, not reconstructed transactions. Practices varied by place and time; no fixed copper–silver rate or universal tax rule is assumed.

Development can mean coexistence. Different forms met different needs. Having more than one form also made conversion and acceptance matters to negotiate. [12]

Epilogue / A familiar question, today

You see a number.
What supports it?

Cash and account balances are familiar forms of money today. A payment app is a way to use or move funds. Imagine buying lunch with PayNow in Singapore, and look beneath the screen. [3] [4]

The visible part

A number on a screen communicates information about a payment. The display alone is not the entire monetary arrangement.

About this consultation demo +

This is a working concept for an individual history project, not the final submission. The proposed learning outcome is to explain changes and coexistence in monetary forms through selected imperial Chinese cases. Song Sichuan is the deepest interactive case, within a wider historical route.

AI assisted with ideation, source discovery, draft wording, code, schematic object drawings, and the two game illustrations. The depicted clothing, buildings, people, and props are interpretive imagery, not independent historical evidence. Sources were opened and checked against the claims used here; the interpretation and final bibliography require the student's review. Development records preserve the key project and image prompts.

The experience runs in your browser, makes no payments, and does not collect visitor responses. Choices are cleared on refresh. Personal video commentary is planned for the final version and has not yet been recorded for this demo.
