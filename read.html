Before 770 BCE

Shang and Western Zhou

Shells could carry value.

Cowrie shells（貝幣）were among the objects used as money in early China. Their shape was also copied in bronze. Money did not begin with one uniform coin: people recognised value in different materials and forms. [9] [10]

More context

The earliest monetary objects existed alongside other ways of exchanging and storing wealth. The shells are an entry into this history, rather than proof that every region followed the same path.

770–221 BCE

Spring and Autumn · Warring States

Different states, different money.

Spade money（布幣）, knife money（刀幣）and round coins circulated in different regions. These forms belonged to a politically divided world. Before imperial unification, there was no single coin standard across all the states. [9] [10]

More context

The photographs show Warring States objects. The different shapes help make regional diversity visible; they were not simply successive steps in a single chain of replacements.

221 BCE–220 CE

Qin and Han

Coins became an imperial standard.

Qin promoted banliang coins（半兩錢）as a common standard. The round coin with a square hole endured, but its weight and rules could change. Under the Han, wuzhu coins（五銖錢）became an important new standard. [9] [11]

“They then cast four-zhu coins, inscribed ‘half-liang’.”

（乃更鑄四銖錢，其文為「半兩」）

Records of the Grand Historian（史記）, vol. 30 [11]
More context

The passage describes a Han coin whose inscription still said “half-liang” after its prescribed weight changed. It shows why an inscription cannot automatically be treated as a measurement. Standardisation connected money with political authority.

Money and state power

The same Han account also describes permission for private casting. The course’s discussion of standardisation helps connect coins with a wider question: who could set the rules of economic life? A recognisable form could endure while rules about weight and production changed. [11] [16]

618–907

Tang

A new name on a familiar shape.

Introduced in 621, Kaiyuan tongbao（開元通寶）moved away from naming coins by weight. Its name referred to circulating treasure. The round, square-holed form continued while the language used to describe money changed. [13]

More context

This coin helps us distinguish a change in naming and monetary conventions from a change in material. Its copper-alloy form was familiar, even as the inscription established a new model for later coinage.

960–1279

Song

Paper was lighter. Trust still mattered.

In Sichuan, heavy iron cash made payments cumbersome. Merchants issued paper jiaozi（交子）, and the government later took over issuance. Paper reduced the burden of carrying coins, but its usefulness depended on acceptance and the issuer’s ability to honour it. [1] [2] [6]

“Iron cash was heavy, inconvenient for trade.”

（鐵錢重，不便貿易）

History of the Song（宋史）, vol. 181 [6]
More context and historical writing

The History of the Song also describes issuers who could not repay their obligations. Compiled under the Yuan, it is a later historical account of the Song. The pictured Northern Song iron coin illustrates the material; it is not a surviving jiaozi note.

From carrying costs to repayment

“They could not repay what they owed.”

（不能償所負）

History of the Song（宋史）, vol. 181 [6]

“They requested a jiaozi bureau in Yizhou.”

（請置益州交子務）

History of the Song（宋史）, vol. 181 [6]

The account brings together metal’s inconvenience, private repayment problems and the creation of an official issuing office. My reading is that monetary innovation shifted the burden: carrying less metal could mean depending more visibly on an issuer. The Song course materials connect commercial expansion with debates over government involvement. [1] [17]

Commerce in Song writing

“Night markets lasted until the third watch.”

（夜市直至三更盡）

Meng Yuanlao, Dreams of the Splendour of the Eastern Capital（東京夢華錄）, vol. 3. [7]

Meng recalls the markets of Kaifeng. In a different city, Liu Yong evokes Hangzhou: “Markets display pearls and gems; households brim with fine silks.” （市列珠璣，戶盈羅綺） [8]

Memoir and poetry bring urban commerce into view. Neither passage tells us how widely jiaozi circulated in Sichuan.

1271–1368

Yuan

Paper moved to the centre of the system.

Kublai introduced a unified paper currency in 1260, before the Yuan dynasty was formally named. Paper became central to the state’s monetary system. As Mongol rule weakened in the fourteenth century, silver displaced notes in private commerce. [14]

More context

The Yuan case extends the Song story: paper was now part of a much wider state monetary project. Its fortunes also depended on political and fiscal conditions, rather than on the material alone.

1368–1912

Ming and Qing

Different forms worked side by side.

The early Ming issued paper notes（大明通行寶鈔）. Silver（白銀）later became important for larger payments and taxation, while copper cash（銅錢）served everyday exchange. Under the Qing, silver and copper continued to meet different needs. [5] [12]

More context

The Ming note pictures strings of coins: one monetary form represented value through another. In the Qing economy, changing between silver and copper mattered because their exchange rate was not fixed everywhere or for all time.

What the Ming note shows

Denomination: the note names one guan（一貫）. Coin imagery: ten groups represent 1,000 cash coins. Printed rules: instructions and a warning against forgery express the issuer’s authority. [5]

My reading is that paper borrowed familiar monetary language to communicate its value. The picture and printed denomination do not, by themselves, establish how much a user could always redeem.

20th–21st centuries

Modern money and payments

A payment moves money through a system.

Modern money includes cash and bank deposits. PayNow, a familiar Singapore example, transfers funds between participating bank or e-wallet accounts. The phone is a way to make a payment; acceptance, institutions and reliable records still matter. [3] [4]

More context

Between the imperial period and today, China moved through silver-based monetary arrangements towards modern fiat currency. PayNow is a comparison from Singapore, not a Chinese currency or a direct descendant of jiaozi. The connection is the continuing question of what makes a payment work. [15]

New forms could solve one problem while creating another. Their usefulness depended on the people and institutions around them.
