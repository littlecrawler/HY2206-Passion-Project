My reflection

Why I chose money, how the website changed, and what HY2206 helped me see.

Watch the project videos

Who is this for?

Students who use money every day but may be new to Chinese history.

What I want to share

Monetary change was shaped by practical needs, political choices and social acceptance. A new form could join older ones, rather than replace them.

From an interest to a website

I first considered two topics: the development of money and the development of architecture. I chose money because it connects ancient objects with something familiar in daily life. Song jiaozi（交子）was my starting point, but I wanted to understand the longer history around it.

A website let me put objects, historical writing and my own explanation together. Based on consultation feedback, I reconsidered the website in this order: audience, message, then form. I focused on students encountering this history for the first time.

Learning to simplify

I initially wanted visible merchants and a game. As the prototype grew, its purpose became harder to follow. Long explanations and too many small headings also asked the reader to do too much at once.

I chose a deck of picture cards so the reader can focus on one period at a time. The first version became too much like a short history summary, so I brought object exploration, comparisons and historical texts back into the cards. I later added a three-dimensional museum while keeping the flashcards and a reading view, so visitors can choose how they explore. I now give the modes different roles: the museum invites close looking, the cards ask focused questions, and the history pages bring together the fuller narrative and historical sources.

Reflections on HY2206

Political authority shapes monetary rules

The course’s discussion of Qin standardisation and the Han Salt and Iron Debate helped me see money as part of governing an empire. [16] The Records of the Grand Historian describes a Han policy that prohibited minting in the commanderies and kingdoms and reserved it to three imperial offices. [11]

I read this as a change in who controlled money. Shared standards could make coins more familiar across regions, while restricting minting concentrated authority in the central government. This changed my idea of monetary “progress”: a reform could solve practical problems and strengthen political control at the same time. Convenience alone is therefore not enough to judge its effects.

Trust depends on promises being kept

I understand trust in money as two connected expectations: that someone else will accept it, and that whoever makes a promise can fulfil it. For a holder of jiaozi, a light note was useful only if it could still be passed on or redeemed.

The Song lectures connect commercial growth with monetary innovation. [17] The History of the Song describes private issuers whose finances weakened until they could no longer repay holders, leading to repeated disputes. [6] My interpretation is that familiar paper could become less dependable when the issuer’s ability to repay changed. Paper reduced the burden of carrying coins, but made its holder more dependent on someone else keeping a promise.

How my understanding changed

I now understand monetary development as changes in convenience, rules and trust. Paper reduced carrying weight; the issuer’s ability to pay still mattered. Modern payment apps also rely on accounts and services to carry out a transaction. [6] [22] [23] The conclusion I draw is that convenience lasts only when the people and institutions involved can do what users expect: honour a note or complete a payment.

Building the website changed how I communicate history, too. Each question needs an answer supported by an object or source. I want visitors to leave with a reason for a historical change, and an understanding of what that change solved or left unresolved.
